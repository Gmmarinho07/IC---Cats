========== ABSTRACT ==========

ABSTRACT: The mechanism of ethanol upgrading to higher
products is still under debate, especially regarding intermediate
species and hydrogenation and dehydrogenation steps. In this
work, we conducted a combined theoretical and experimental
approach to contribute to this discussion. For such, detailed
electronic structure density functional theory calculations (aiming
at probing density of states, infrared spectra, geometric parameters,
charge densities, and reaction energetics) and diﬀuse reﬂectance
infrared Fourier transform spectroscopy experiments were carried
out revealing the relevance of an appropriate combination of
reactive surface sites to support the formation of several
intermediates that are formed in the C−C coupling over MgO.
The roles of Mg and O sites were also studied under an electronic perspective and diﬀerent geometrical arrangements. We found
that a kink conﬁguration was the most adequate for ethanol to 1-butanol upgrading. Our calculations also gave us arguments to
propose distinct reaction routes, whose mutual predominance would depend upon reaction temperature. At temperatures up to 573
K, the so-called β-route, which goes through scission of a Cβ−H bond and formation of an oxametallacycle-like intermediate, would
dominate the coupling, whereas at higher temperatures, up to 673 K, a more usual Guerbet mechanism, via an aldol coupling step
and then consecutive hydrogenations, would be expected. The theoretical conclusions were followed by a careful experimental
strategy using sequential experimental planning techniques in order to estimate accurate parameters with the lowest possible
experimental load. Information from these diﬀerent sources was coupled to develop a mathematical model for the rate of the ethanol
upgrading reaction, using a Langmuir−Hinshelwood−Hougen−Watson approach. The developed and statistically validated model

MgO catalyst was prepared via
precipitation of Mg(OH)2 by the addition of a solution of
KOH and K2CO3 to Mg(NO3)2. Mg(OH)2 was then thermally
treated to yield the used MgO. The material was characterized
by X-ray ﬂuorescence (XRF), X-ray diﬀraction (XRD), N2
physisorption, and CO2 temperature-programmed desorption
(TPD). For full synthesis and characterization descriptions, the
reader is kindly referred to our previous work.56
2.4.2. Diﬀuse Reﬂectance Infrared Fourier Transform
Spectroscopy. The tests were conducted in a Bruker VERTEX
70, with a liquid nitrogen-cooled MCT detector, ZnSe
windows, and a diﬀuse reﬂectance chamber for high-temper-
ature treatments (Harrick). The samples were pretreated at
573 K in 30 mL/min of pure Ar for 1 h and then cooled back
to 323 K. Afterward, the catalyst was submitted to a 30 mL/
min 1% EtOH in Ar ﬂow at 323 K prior to recording spectra
every 5 min for 30 min. Then, the ﬂow was switched back to
pure Ar to assess the behavior of EtOH desorption. The
spectra were also obtained every 5 min for 30 min after pure Ar
started ﬂowing. This procedure was repeated up to 673 K with
temperature increments of 50 K.
2.4.3. Bench-Scale Kinetic Experiments. An Agilent 7890A
gas chromatograph with an FID detector was used to conduct
the experiments. All species were detected using an Agilent
CP-PoraPLOT QH column (25 m, 0.32 mm i.d.). Nitrogen
(Praxair, ultra-high purity) was used as carrier gas and methane
(Praxair, ultra-high purity) was used as internal standard at a
3% concentration. Anhydrous EtOH (Sigma-Aldrich, >99.5%)
was fed into the system using a Teledyne ISCO 500D syringe
pump. An evaporator kept at 413 K was used to vaporize the
incoming EtOH prior to the mix with the carrier gas. A 1/2″
tubular Ti reactor was used for all reactions. Internal pressure
build-up was monitored using a pressure gauge and the
reaction temperature was monitored using a thermocouple
inserted into the tube at the catalyst bed height. The lines were
