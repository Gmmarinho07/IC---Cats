selectivity up to 72.1 % was firstly achieved. This work gains new insight 
to mild synthesis of alternative higher alcohols from readily-accessible 
aqueous bio-ethanol and the waste-to-resource strategy.

========== EXPERIMENTAL ==========

Experimental section 
2.1. Materials 
All chemicals were commercially available and used as purchased 
without further purification. Nickel (II) nitrate (Ni(NO3)2⋅6H2O, 99.8 %) 
was purchased from Macklin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. (China). 
Melt-blown cloth (MB) was obtained from Shixin Textile Company 
(China), with a basis weight ranging from 18 to 60 g/m2 and average 
molecular weight between 40,000 and 90,000. Citric acid (C6H8O7⋅H2O, 
CA, with a purity specification of 99.5 %) was purchased from Aladdin 
Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. (China). 
2.2. Catalyst preparation 
The Ni@MB catalysts were fabricated through a facile impregnation- 
carbonization strategy using melt-blown cloth (MB) and Ni(NO3)2⋅6H2O 
as staring materials as shown in Fig. 1. Firstly, the Ni(NO3)2⋅6H2O (1.6 
g) was added into certain amount of ethanol to obtain a homogeneous 
nickel-ethanol solution. Then, the MB (unit weight of 25 g/m2, 1.5 g) 
was fragmented and impregnated into the above nickel-ethanol solution 
with thoroughly stirring, followed by drying overnight at 80 ◦C in oven. 
Subsequently, the dried precursor was transferred to a tubular furnace 
and carbonized for 2 h at certain temperature with N2 flow. Finally, the 
Ni@MB catalyst was obtained as black sample. The catalysts prepared 
with various conditions were denoted as Ni@MB-X-T, where X repre­
sented the molar ratio of Ni to MB (MB was calculated with PP basis, 
typical ratios of 1/5, 1/10, 1/15, 1/20, 1/25, 1/30, 1/35 and 1/50 were 
adopted), and T represented the carbonization temperature (typical 
temperatures of 400, 450, 500, 550, 600, 650 and 700 ◦C were adopted). 
For comparison, the Ni@CA catalyst was also prepared using same 
method except adopting citric acid (CA) as staring material instead of 
MB.

2.3. Catalyst characterization 
The crystal states of obtained catalysts were analyzed by using X-ray 
diffractometer (MiniFlex 600, Rigaku, Ltd., Japan) equipped with a Cu 
Kα radiation source and a wavelength (λ) of 1.5418 nm. The 2θ scanning 
range was set from 5◦to 80◦, with a scanning rate of 10◦/min. The 
microstructure and morphology were observed using scanning electron 
microscopy (SEM, SU8220, Hitachi) and transmission electron micro­
scopy (TEM, FEI Thermo Talos F200S TEM). Prior to the characteriza­
tion, the samples were thoroughly ground in an agate mortar. After 30 
min of ultrasonic dispersion in anhydrous ethanol, the dispersed solu­
tion was dropped onto a carbon film supported on a copper grid for 
observation. The Brunauer-Emmett-Teller (BET) method was used to 
measure the pore characteristics and specific surface area of the mate­
rials at Micromeritics ASAP 2460. Prior to measurement, the samples 
were first degassed at 300 ◦C for 12 h. The Barrett-Joyner-Halenda 
(BJH) method was employed for further analysis of the data. Thermo 
Scientific K-Alpha X-ray Photoelectron Spectroscopy (XPS) analyzer was 
adopted to analyze the surface elemental composition and chemical 
states of the samples. Approximately 20 mg of sample was pressed into a 
pellet for testing, the acquired data was calibrated based on the binding 
energy of the C 1 s peak at 284.8 eV. The CO2 temperature-programmed 
desorption-mass spectrometry (CO2-TPD-MS) experiment was conduct­
ed to analyze the desorption process of carbon dioxide on the catalyst.

According to the catalytic performances of ethanol aqueous coupling 
shown in Fig. 7, the trends of ethanol conversion and higher alcohols 
yield varied with the change of Ni/MB ratio in Ni@MB-X-500 catalyst. 
When the Ni/MB ratio decreased from 1/5 to 1/20, the ethanol con­
version increased from 64.6 % to 74.1 %, and the yield of higher alco­
hols also increased from 23.8 % to 46.9 %. More importantly, we found 
that the carbon distribution of higher alcohols shifted significantly to­
wards C6 + higher alcohols, with the highest selectivity reaching 66.7 
%. In general, the carbon distribution of higher alcohol products from 
ethanol coupling usually shows decreasing trend with the increase of 
carbon number due to the step-growth cascade reaction process (Eagan 
et al., 2020). Here, the selectivity of C6 hexanol reached 28.6 % 
compared with 29.9 % of C4 butanol over the Ni@MB-1/20–500 cata­
lyst as shown in Fig. 7b, which clearly broke the traditional cascading 
chain growth pattern of carbon distribution. This should be caused by 
the introduction of additional basic centers by N-doping, which en­
hances the aldehydes coupling (Ling et al., 2023; Juwen et al., 2022; Lin 
et al., 2022). Further decreased the Ni/MB ratio from 1/20 to 1/35, the 
conversion of ethanol and higher alcohols yield decreased accordingly.

3.2.2. Effect of carbonization temperature on Ni@MB catalytic activity 
The ethanol coupling catalytic activity using Ni@MB-1/20-T cata­
lysts prepared at different carbonization temperatures were shown in 
Fig. 8. It can be observed that both ethanol conversion and higher al­
cohols yield enhanced as the catalyst carbonization temperature 
increased from 400 ◦C to 500 ◦C. But further increased the carbonization 
temperature to 700 ◦C led to significant decay of catalyst activity. Based 
on prior XRD and XPS analysis, the low carbonization temperature like 
400 ◦C is inadequate for the formation of active-Ni and the over-high 
carbonization temperature led to severe metal agglomeration, which 
responsible for their declined catalyst activities. Optimally, the Ni@MB- 
1/20–500 catalyst exhibited the best activity with ethanol conversion 
reached 74.1 %. 
3.2.3. Effect of reaction temperature 
The reaction temperature usually play crucial role for catalytic per­
formance and the low reaction temperature is desired for its benefits in 
energy conservation and low reactor cost. Herein, the effect of reaction 
temperature on ethanol coupling to higher alcohols was investigated 
over the optimal Ni@MB-1/20–500 catalyst. As results shown in Fig. 9, 
the ethanol conversion increased significantly from 32.9 % to 93.3 % as 
the reaction temperature increased from 140 ◦C to 240 ◦C. However, the 
selectivity of undesired gas product 

 coupling to higher alcohols was investigated 
over the optimal Ni@MB-1/20–500 catalyst. As results shown in Fig. 9, 
the ethanol conversion increased significantly from 32.9 % to 93.3 % as 
the reaction temperature increased from 140 ◦C to 240 ◦C. However, the 
selectivity of undesired gas product also expanded with increased re­
action temperature, which caused by enhanced C-C cleavage side re­
actions. At reaction temperatures of 240 ◦C, the gas by-product 
selectivity reached 50.7 %, which led to poor carbon atom efficiency 
toward target higher alcohols. Preferably, the Ni@MB-1/20–500 cata­
lyst exhibited the optimal higher alcohols yield of 46.9 % and C6 +
higher alcohols selectivity of 66.7 % at 170 ◦C reaction temperature.
