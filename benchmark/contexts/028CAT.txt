========== EXPERIMENTAL ==========

Experimental
2.1. Catalysts preparation
2.1.1. Preparation of NiMgAlO and CuMgAlO LDHs
All LDHs were prepared using a co-precipitation method. Taking
the NiMgAlO LDHs as an example, 50 mL aqueous solution contain-
ing 0.02 mol Ni(NO3)26H2O, 0.08 mol Mg(NO3)26H2O, and
0.02 mol Al(NO3)39H2O was dropwise added to a ﬁercely stirred
50 mL solution containing 0.08 mol Na2CO3 and 0.12 mol NaOH.
The pH of the obtained mixture was adjusted to 10 by a 3 mol/L
NaOH solution. The mixture was kept at 338 K for 18 h, and a
hydrotalcite structure of NiMgAlO precursor was formed. The
hydrotalcite was ﬁltered, rinsed with 500 mL deionized water,
dried at 393 K for 12 h, and ﬁnally got the LDHs of NiMgAlO with
a Ni:Mg:Al molar ratio of 1:4:1.
2.1.2. Preparation of NiMgAlO and CuMgAlO catalysts
The obtained LDHs of NiMgAlO and CuMgAlO were calcined at
873 K in the air for 2 h, reduced at 873 K in hydrogen for 1 h,
and obtained NiMgAlO and CuMgAlO catalysts.
2.1.3. Preparation of Cu-NiMgAlO catalysts
Cu-NiMgAlO catalysts were prepared by the metal ion adsorp-
tion method [36]. In a typical run, 20 mL aqueous solution contain-
ing a certain amount of Cu(NO3)23H2O was dropwise added to
50 mL ﬁercely stirred solution containing 0.5 g LDHs of NiMgAlO,
and further stirred at 338 K for 1 h. Then, the pH of obtained mix-
ture was adjusted to 10 by 0.2 mol/L NaOH solution, and main-
tained ﬁercely stirring for 4 h. After the ﬁltration process, the
resulted slurry was washed with 100 mL deionized water, and
dried at 393 K for 12 h. Finally, the obtained powder was calcinated
at 873 K for 2 h, reduced at 873 K in hydrogen for 1 h, and yielded
Cu-NiMgAlO catalysts. The number before Cu was the theoretical
amount of Cu loading calculated based on the amount of

2.2. Characterizations
X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns of different NiMgAlO precur-
sors and catalysts were performed on a PANalytical X’Pert-Pro
powder X-ray diffractometer using Cu Ka monochromated radia-
tion (k = 0.15418 nm) at 40 kV and 40 mA. Before analysis, samples
were ground to powder larger than 100 meshes, and loaded onto a
glass slide. The patterns were recorded in 2h range 10–80 at a
scan speed of 5 /min. PANalytical X’Pert HighScore Plus software
was used for qualitative analysis.
The pore structure of NiMgAlO based catalysts was measured
using a Micromeritics ASAP 2020 system with nitrogen as the
adsorbent at 77 K. Prior to nitrogen adsorption, about 0.1 g catalyst
was out-gassed at 393 K for 3 h, and then at 523 K for 3 h. The
speciﬁc surface area was determined by the BET method, pore vol-
ume and average pore diameter were calculated by the BJH
method.
High-angle annular dark ﬁeld scanning transmission electron
microscopy (HAADF-STEM) and energy dispersive spectroscopy
(EDS) elemental mapping were conducted on a JEM-2100F micro-
scope. Before analysis, the sample was ground and dispersed in
ethanol under ultrasonic conditions for 10 min. The dispersed sam-
ple was then mounted onto carbon-coated Mo grids followed by
slow evaporation of ethanol at room temperature.
X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) of different catalysts
was recorded on a ThermoFisher spectrometer using Al Ka
(ht = 1486.6 eV) X-ray source with a pass energy of 30 eV, and a
base pressure of 5109 mbar in analysis chamber. The spectra
before and after 10 s Ar etching were collected. Binding energy
(BE) values were referenced to the binding energy of the C 1s core
level at 284.6 eV.

Results and discussion
3.1. Catalytic conversion of ethanol over different catalysts
3.1.1. Optimize metal-doped NiMgAlO catalysts for ethanol conversion
Different metals were introduced into the hydrotalcite of NiM-
gAlO by the metal ion adsorption method, and used for ethanol
conversions. As shown in Fig. 1(a), over the benchmark catalyst
of NiMgAlO, ethanol conversion and n-butanol selectivity were
ca. 18% and 55.4%, respectively, at 503 K for 24 h. After introducing
metals of Fe, Co, Cu, Pt, Pd, Rh and Ir, distinct performances were
observed for the conversion of ethanol to n-butanol. The introduc-
tion of Fe and Rh slightly decreased the ethanol conversion due to
their insufﬁcient activity in dehydrogenation reactions, and the
presence of Fe, Pt, Rh and Ir reduced the n-butanol selectivity to
39–48% because of their high activity in C–C bond cleavages
[38,39]. In contrast, Co, Cu and Pd improved both ethanol conver-
sion and n-butanol selectivity. Speciﬁcally, over the 1%Cu-NiMgAlO

Ethanol dehydrogenation is the ﬁrst and rate-determining step
for the conversion of ethanol to n-butanol [56]. Compared with the
NiMgAlO catalyst, the presence of Cu greatly increased the number
of metal active sites and enhanced hydrogen transfer activity [57].
As a result, the ethanol dehydrogenation reaction was accelerated,
and the ethanol conversion was greatly improved, as shown in
Fig. 1. On the other hand, when the Cu loading was overmuch,
e.g. the 4%Cu-NiMgAlO catalyst, bulk CuNi alloys were formed,
which led to a decrease of the dehydrogenation activity and low
ethanol conversions.
